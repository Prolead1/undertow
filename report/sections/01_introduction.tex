\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{utomated} market makers (AMMs) turned market making into a
public utility. A liquidity provider (LP) deposits a pair of assets into a
smart contract, and the contract prices every trade through a deterministic
trading function. Uniswap~v3 refined that bargain with
\emph{concentrated liquidity}: an LP chooses a price interval, and the
deposited capital backs trades only inside it~\cite{adams2021uniswap}. Because
fee accrual is proportional to the share of active liquidity, narrowing the
interval raises the fee earned per dollar of capital while the price stays
inside the band. This is the promise that drew capital to v3: the same deposit
can quote with ten, a hundred, or a thousand times the fee density of a
full-range position.

The catch is that liquidity quoted at a stale price is an open invitation to
traders with better information. Every arbitrage trade that moves the pool
price toward the market price is a trade against the LP, and the resulting loss
does not reverse when the price moves back. Milionis et al.\ formalized this
cost of adverse selection as \emph{loss-versus-rebalancing} (LVR): the shortfall
of a constant-product LP position relative to a hypothetical portfolio that
continuously rebalances to the same token mix~\cite{milionis2022lvr}. For a
full-range constant-product pool, LVR accrues at one eighth of the square of
volatility times the position value, a rate that grows with the very activity
that generates fees. The empirical record is unfavorable. Fritsch and Canidio
compare the arbitrage losses large AMM pools actually realized with the fee
income those pools produced: in their sample a passive full-range deposit would
have outperformed the concentrated alternatives~\cite{fritsch2024measuring}.
Loesch et al.\ reach a related conclusion
from v3 positions directly: concentration magnifies impermanent loss when the
price leaves the band, so a narrow range can underperform even a v2
deposit~\cite{loesch2021impermanent}. Managing a range is not free either:
hitting the edges forces a recentring trade that pays pool fees plus
slippage, and each action costs gas.

The LP therefore faces a sequential decision problem, not a portfolio choice.
At every decision instant it must decide \emph{whether} to act, \emph{where} to
place the next band and \emph{how wide} to make it, and \emph{how much} of the
position's directional exposure to hedge. The levers interact: a wider band
earns less per trade but defers the next costly recentring; a hedge removes
directional variance but pays funding and perp fees; acting sooner protects fee
income but multiplies transaction costs. The heuristics in common use (recenter
when the price exits the band, rebalance when the expected fee gain covers gas,
hold a wide static band~\cite{adams2021uniswap}) are single points in this
space, chosen by argument rather than learned from outcomes.

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a natural fit for the joint problem, and recent
work has begun to explore it. Zhang et al.\ train a hedged double deep
Q-network agent for v3 liquidity provision and report sizeable gains over
baselines, but their hedge executes without cost or funding, and the policy
sees no regime features~\cite{zhang2023adaptive}. ZeroSwap learns to set the
spread of a market maker so that it tracks an external price, an orthogonal
lever to range placement, and targets zero profit against competitive
counterparties~\cite{nadkarni2023zeroswap}. RAmmStein derives rebalancing
thresholds that depend on the regime through impulse control and reports that a
policy which rebalances lazily when the regime warrants it improves net ROI
while cutting rebalance counts by roughly $85\,\%$, but its net metric excludes
impermanent loss, it studies a single pair, and it relies on off-chain market
data~\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}. Chionas et al.\ study concentrated liquidity
as a problem of risk aversion in simulation and find that more volatile and
more expensive environments push optimal positions wider and less frequently
rebalanced, with aversion to risk compressing the left tail of returns; their
analysis, however, is simulation only, uses no on-chain data, and leaves the
hedge leg open~\cite{chionas2026concentrated}. What is missing is a study that
(i) evaluates policies on \emph{on-chain} replay, (ii) charges every
friction (gas, swap fees, slippage, perp costs and funding) on both the LP and
hedge legs, (iii) optimizes and reports Sharpe and CVaR rather than raw profit,
and (iv) treats rebalance timing, band width and hedge ratio as one joint
action.

This paper builds that study around four claims.
\begin{itemize}
\item[\textbf{C1}] \textbf{Limits.} On the pools and windows we study, passive
fee income alone does not cover the sum of LVR, impermanent loss and rebalancing
costs; we quantify the decomposition and the cost of greedy rebalancing,
extending the LVR literature with an exact on-chain replay engine.
\item[\textbf{C2}] \textbf{Failure modes.} Naive RL fails on this Markov
decision process (MDP). We document degenerate policies that collapse to a
single action, identify what breaks (an observation in which the live band is
hidden, a reward whose benchmark drifts away from the reported metric after a
rebalance, and training windows that never change), and show that each fix (band geometry
in the observation, an excess-versus-HODL reward, and window resampling) is
necessary.
\item[\textbf{C3}] \textbf{A learned joint policy.} A risk-sensitive policy
that controls band timing, band width and hedge ratio jointly beats the
strongest family of rules tuned by hand on data held out from training, and the
way it splits on each feature recovers the underlying economics: band exit, the
trade-off between fees and gas, and volatility regime.
\item[\textbf{C4}] \textbf{The active/passive boundary.} We map the region in
which active management beats a passive or hedged HODL benchmark as a function
of volatility, fee tier, volume relative to liquidity, gas price and capital.
\end{itemize}

The claims are ordered deliberately: C1 establishes the economic terrain, C2
explains why the obvious RL approach fails there, and C3--C4 show what a policy
that survives the terrain can do. To measure all four against ground truth, we
built \emph{Undertow}, a testbed that couples an exact replay backtester over
two years of on-chain Uniswap~v3 events, with fee accounting kept in integer
arithmetic, to a fast float64 Gymnasium simulator, and we place the two engines
under a parity harness so that the simulator can be trusted where it is cheap
and checked where it matters~\cite{towers2024gymnasium}.
