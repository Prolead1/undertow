\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Automated market makers allow any participant to earn trading fees without
quoting prices by hand. Uniswap~v3 refined the offer: a liquidity provider (LP)
selects the price range in which it is willing to trade, so a deposit of
\usdc{100000} earns the fee share of a larger full-range position while the
price stays inside the chosen band\cite{adams2021uniswap}. Narrowing the band
raises the fee earned per dollar of capital, and that prospect drew liquidity
into v3.

The promise hides a cost. Liquidity quoted away from the market price is an
offer that arbitrageurs accept, and such trades push the pool toward the market
price while leaving the LP holding more of the falling asset. Because the loss
is a transfer rather than a fluctuation, it does not reverse when the price
returns. Milionis et al.\ named this adverse selection cost
loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) and showed that a full-range position loses value
at a rate proportional to the square of volatility\cite{milionis2022lvr}.
Fritsch and Canidio measured realised arbitrage losses on large pools and found
that they exceed the fees those pools earn\cite{fritsch2024measuring}, and
Loesch et al.\ showed that concentration magnifies the loss once the price
leaves the band\cite{loesch2021impermanent}. Fee income alone is not the LP's
return.

An LP who seeks more than a passive range faces three decisions that interact,
so they cannot be made independently. When to recentre trades fee income against
gas and slippage; how wide to set the next band trades fee density against the
time until an edge; how much of the position to hedge trades directional risk
against funding and perp fees. These choices arrive as costly impulses under
uncertainty about volatility, order flow and regime. The objective is not the
highest fee income but the highest return per unit of risk, which places the
problem within risk-sensitive impulse control rather than static portfolio
choice\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}.

Recent work addresses parts of the problem. Zhang et al.\ train a hedged agent
for v3 provision, but the hedge executes without cost or funding and the policy
sees no regime information\cite{zhang2023adaptive}. ZeroSwap learns to set a
market maker's spread so that quoted prices track an external reference, a
different lever from range placement\cite{nadkarni2023zeroswap}. RAmmStein
derives regime-dependent rebalancing thresholds and reports fewer rebalances
for the same net return, but its net metric excludes impermanent loss and it
works with off-chain data for a single pair\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}. Chionas
et al.\ study risk aversion in concentrated liquidity inside a simulation and
leave out on-chain data and the hedge\cite{chionas2026concentrated}. None of
these places timing, width and hedge decisions in a single risk-sensitive
policy, charges the frictions on both the liquidity and hedge legs, and tests
the result on recorded on-chain trades against hand-written rules.

This report closes that gap. The evidence base is a testbed built from three
years of recorded trades in two real ETH/USDC pools. An exact replay backtester
computes fees in the protocol's own integer arithmetic, a fast simulator
supports training, and a parity harness keeps the two in agreement on identical
inputs. On that testbed we ask four questions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Where do fees fail?} We decompose passive returns into fees,
impermanent loss, gas and slippage, and identify the conditions under which the
deficit persists.
\item \textbf{Why did the first policies fail?} We document the collapse of a
standard policy on this task, trace it to three interface problems, and
quantify what the repairs recover.
\item \textbf{Can one policy do better?} We train a risk-sensitive policy over
timing, width and hedging and compare it out of sample with the strongest
hand-written rules and a delta-matched hedge.
\item \textbf{Where does active management pay?} We map the boundary between
active and passive provision across volatility, fee tier, gas and capital.
\end{itemize}

The failures of the first policies are part of the result, not a footnote. In
this domain, what the agent is allowed to see and what it is rewarded for
matter more than the choice of learning algorithm. A report that showed only
the eventual policy would hide the more transferable lesson.
