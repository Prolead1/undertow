\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{utomated} market makers let anyone earn trading fees without
quoting prices by hand. Uniswap~v3 sharpened the offer: an LP chooses the price
range it is willing to trade in, so a deposit of \usdc{100000} can earn the
same fees as a much larger full-range position as long as the price stays
inside the chosen band\cite{adams2021uniswap}. Narrowing the band raises the
fee earned per dollar of capital, and that promise is what drew liquidity
into v3.

The promise has a hidden price. Liquidity quoted at a price that has already
moved is an offer that arbitrageurs will take. Every such trade pushes the pool
toward the market price and leaves the LP holding more of the asset that is
falling and less of the one that is rising. The loss does not reverse when the
price comes back, because it is a transfer rather than a fluctuation. Milionis
et al.\ gave this adverse selection cost its modern name, loss-versus-rebalancing
(LVR), and showed that a full-range position loses value at a rate proportional
to the square of volatility\cite{milionis2022lvr}. Fritsch and Canidio then
measured realised arbitrage losses across large pools and found that they
exceed the fees those pools earn\cite{fritsch2024measuring}. Loesch et al.\
showed that concentration makes matters worse when the price leaves the band,
since a narrow position can lose more than a plain full-range
deposit\cite{loesch2021impermanent}. Fee income, on its own, is not the LP's
return.

An LP who wants more than a passive range has three decisions to make, and they
interact. The first is when to recenter: acting early protects fee income, while
waiting saves gas and slippage. The second is how wide to set the next band: a
narrow band earns more per trade but leaves it sooner. The third is how much of
the position's price exposure to hedge: a short position in the perpetual
market removes directional swings, but it costs fees and funding. Each decision
is an impulse, lumpy in cost and taken under uncertainty about volatility,
order flow and regime. The objective is not simply to earn more, but to earn
more per unit of risk taken. The decisions are lumpy in cost and made under
uncertainty, which makes this a problem of impulse control rather than a
static portfolio choice\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}.

Learning these decisions is a natural application of reinforcement learning,
and recent work takes up parts of it. Zhang et al.\ train a hedged agent for v3
liquidity provision, but their hedge executes without cost or funding and the
policy sees no regime information\cite{zhang2023adaptive}. ZeroSwap learns to
set a market maker's spread so that it tracks an external price, which is a
different lever: it decides how wide the quoted price is, not where liquidity
sits\cite{nadkarni2023zeroswap}. RAmmStein derives rebalancing thresholds that
depend on the regime from a model of impulse control and reports large savings in
rebalance count, but its net return excludes impermanent loss and it works with
off-chain data for a single pair\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}. Chionas et al.\
study risk aversion in concentrated liquidity within a simulation and find that
volatile, expensive markets push optimal bands wider and rebalances rarer, but
they use no on-chain data and leave the hedge out\cite{chionas2026concentrated}.
None of these studies places the timing, width and hedge decisions in a single
risk-sensitive policy, charges every friction on both the liquidity and hedge
legs, and evaluates it on recorded on-chain trades against rules written by
hand.

This report does that. We build a testbed on two years of recorded trades from
two real ETH/USDC pools, with two engines: an exact replay backtester that
computes fees in the protocol's own integer arithmetic, and a fast simulator
that can be trained on for millions of steps. A parity harness compares the two
on identical inputs, so the simulator can be trusted where speed is needed and
checked where accuracy matters. The report then asks four questions, in order.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Where do fees fail?} We decompose passive returns into fees,
impermanent loss, gas and slippage, and locate the conditions under which the
losses win.
\item \textbf{Why does naive learning fail?} We document the collapse of a
standard policy on this task, trace it to three interface problems, and measure
what each repair buys.
\item \textbf{Can one policy do better?} We train a risk-sensitive policy over
timing, width and hedging and compare it out of sample with the best
hand-written rules and a hedged benchmark.
\item \textbf{Where is active management worth it?} We map the boundary between
active and passive provision across volatility, fee tier, gas and capital.
\end{itemize}

The failures of the first policies are part of the result, not a footnote. A
report that showed only the eventual policy would hide the most transferable
lesson: in this domain, what the agent is allowed to see and what it is rewarded
for matter more than the choice of learning algorithm.
