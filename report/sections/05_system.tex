\section{Building a Trustworthy Testbed}
\label{sec:system}

A study of this question needs a world in which the LP's costs can be measured
rather than assumed. That world must be exact enough that a reported fee or
loss is the real one, and fast enough that a policy can make millions of
decisions. No single engine satisfies both, so the testbed uses two and checks
them against each other. Figure~\ref{fig:testbed} shows the flow.

\subsection{The record}

The data are the raw logs of two real ETH/USDC pools: the $0.05\,\%$ pool used
as the primary market and the $0.30\,\%$ pool used as a fee-tier contrast. The
pipeline turns those logs into one ordered event tape of swaps, mints, burns
and collects, and joins it with three auxiliary feeds: one-minute reference
prices, per-block gas prices, and regime labels computed from thresholds fixed
in advance. A fourth feed, the funding rate of a perpetual margined in USDT,
prices the hedge. The window runs from January 2022 to December 2024, with
2022--2023 for training and 2024 held out. The primary pool's training tape
alone holds about 4.86 million events, which is why speed matters later. Every
read goes through one interface that enforces block ordering and the train/eval
boundary, so an observation at time $t$ cannot accidentally see a bar from
$t+1$.

\subsection{The exact engine}

The backtester replays a frozen policy against the tape in the protocol's own
integer arithmetic. Fees come from the pool's recorded fee-growth accumulators,
exactly as the contract computes them; liquidity events update an external tick
lattice seeded from a boundary snapshot; swaps are apportioned across the ticks
they cross. The ledger therefore satisfies an accounting identity by
construction: fees plus impermanent loss, minus gas and slippage, equals the
strategy's excess over holding the opening deposit. A backtested policy is
frozen by construction because the interface exposes no way to update it, and
every ledger records the configuration and seed that produced it.

\subsection{Two engines, one check}

Training on the exact engine would be far too slow, so the simulator mirrors
the same economics in floating point. Its agent is marginal: the position never
moves the price, and its fee share is the fraction of active liquidity it
supplies. The simulator can run on the recorded events or on a calibrated
regime-switching jump process fitted to the training years, and it executes
each action at the price observed before the interval advances, as a live LP
would.

Two numeric worlds are only useful if their disagreement is bounded. The parity
harness feeds both engines the same prices, positions and gas series, and then
compares fees, impermanent loss and PnL under a stated tolerance. Since the
simulator's fee engine was made exact, the remaining drift on real data is
tiny: mean fee differences on the order of $10^{-7}$ USDC per step per seed,
and impermanent loss matching exactly. The harness originally exercised only the
passive policy, which never rebalances; it now also runs a policy that does,
which is the path the fee and loss results depend on. A separate suite of
look-ahead probes attacks the composed system at the train/eval seam.

\subsection{The failures that shaped the design}

The first two training runs produced policies that did nothing useful: every
seed collapsed to one deterministic action, some always holding and some always
repeating a single rebalance, and the measured advantage barely distinguished
the actions. The collapse was not a tuning problem. Table~\ref{tab:repairs}
lists the interface defects behind it and the repair for each. Each repair is
small, but together they were the difference between a policy that can learn
and one that cannot, which is why the failure is treated as a result rather
than an implementation note.
