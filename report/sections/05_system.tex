\section{Building a Trustworthy Testbed}
\label{sec:system}

A study of this question needs a world in which the LP's costs can be measured
rather than assumed. That world has to meet two demands at once: it must be
exact enough that a reported fee or loss is the real one, and fast enough that a
policy can make millions of decisions. No single engine satisfies both, so the
testbed uses two and checks them against each other.

\subsection{The record}

The data are the raw logs of two real ETH/USDC pools: the $0.05\,\%$ pool used
as the primary market, and the $0.30\,\%$ pool used as a fee-tier contrast. The
pipeline turns those logs into one ordered event tape of swaps, mints, burns and
collects, and joins it with three auxiliary feeds: one-minute reference prices,
per-block gas prices, and regime labels computed from thresholds fixed in
advance. A fourth feed, the funding rate of a perpetual margined in USDT, prices the
hedge. The window runs from January 2022 to December 2024. Training uses 2022 and
2023; 2024 is held out. The primary pool's training tape alone holds about 4.86
million events, which is why speed matters so much later. Every read goes
through one interface that enforces block ordering and the train/eval boundary,
so an observation at time $t$ cannot accidentally see a bar from $t+1$.

\subsection{The exact engine}

The backtester replays a frozen policy against the tape in the protocol's own
integer arithmetic. Fees come from the pool's recorded fee-growth accumulators,
exactly as the contract computes them; liquidity events update an external tick
lattice seeded from a boundary snapshot; swaps are apportioned across the ticks
they cross. The ledger therefore satisfies an accounting identity by
construction: fees plus impermanent loss, minus gas and slippage, equals the
strategy's excess over holding the opening deposit. A backtested
policy is frozen by construction because the interface exposes no way to update
it, and every ledger records the configuration and seed that produced it.

\subsection{Two engines, one check}

Training on the exact engine would be far too slow, so the simulator mirrors the
same economics in floating point. Its agent is marginal: the position never
moves the price, and its fee share is the fraction of active liquidity it
supplies. The simulator can run on the recorded events or on a calibrated
regime-switching jump process fitted to the training years, and it executes each
action at the price observed before the interval advances, as a live LP would.

Two numeric worlds are only useful if their disagreement is bounded. The parity
harness feeds both engines the same prices, positions and gas series, and then
compares fees, impermanent loss and PnL under a stated tolerance. Since the
simulator's fee engine was made exact, the remaining drift on real data is tiny:
mean fee differences on the order of $10^{-7}$ of notional per seed, and
impermanent loss matching exactly. The harness originally exercised only the
passive policy, which never rebalances; it now also runs a policy that does,
which is the path the fee and loss results depend on. A separate suite of
look-ahead probes attacks the composed system at the train/eval seam.

\subsection{The failures that shaped the design}

The first two training runs produced policies that did nothing useful. Every
seed collapsed to one deterministic action, some always holding and some always
repeating a single rebalance, and the measured advantage barely distinguished
the actions. The collapse was not a tuning problem. The observation contained no
information about the band, so a policy could not tell how close the price was
to an edge or how far a recentring would have to move. The reward measured the
loss against a rolling entry basket, so after a rebalance it no longer matched
the metric the evaluation reported. And every training episode replayed the same
window of thirty days, so the agent never saw a different market. Two cost defects
surfaced while fixing those: slippage was charged on the whole position value
instead of the inventory actually swapped, and the marginal fee credit was not
capped when the agent supplied only a small share of liquidity.

Each repair is small. Together they were the difference between a policy that
can learn and one that cannot, which is why the failure itself is treated as a
result rather than an implementation note.
