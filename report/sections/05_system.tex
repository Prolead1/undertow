\section{Building a Trustworthy Testbed}
\label{sec:system}

The decision problem framed in Section~\ref{sec:formulation} needs a world in
which the LP's costs are measured rather than assumed. Such a world must be
exact enough that a reported fee or loss is the real one, and fast enough for a
learned policy to make millions of decisions. No single engine delivers both,
so the testbed runs two and checks one against the other. Constructing that
instrument is part of the contribution, because a result is only as
trustworthy as the recording and the arithmetic beneath it.
Figure~\ref{fig:testbed} shows the flow.

\subsection{The record}

The record begins with the raw logs of two real ETH/USDC pools. The
$0.05\,\%$ pool is the primary pool, and the $0.30\,\%$ pool provides a
fee-tier contrast. The pipeline turns those logs into one ordered event tape of
swaps, mints, burns and collects, then joins three auxiliary feeds to it,
namely one-minute reference prices, per-block gas prices and regime labels
computed from thresholds fixed in advance. A fourth feed, the funding rate of a
perpetual margined in USDT, prices the hedge. The window runs from January 2022
to December 2024, with 2022--2023 for training and 2024 held out. The primary
pool's training tape alone holds about 4.86 million events, which is why speed
matters later. Reads pass through one interface that enforces block ordering
and the train/eval boundary, so an observation at time $t$ cannot accidentally
see a bar from $t+1$.

\subsection{The exact engine}

The exact backtester replays a frozen policy against the tape in the protocol's
own integer arithmetic. Fees come from the pool's recorded fee-growth
accumulators, exactly as the contract computes them. Liquidity events update an
external tick lattice seeded from a boundary snapshot, and swaps are
apportioned across the ticks they cross. The ledger therefore satisfies its
accounting identity exactly. Fees plus impermanent loss, minus gas and
slippage, equals the strategy's excess over holding the opening deposit.
Because the interface exposes no way to update the policy, a backtested run is
frozen by construction, and the ledger records the configuration and seed
behind it.

\subsection{Two engines, one check}

Training on the exact backtester would be far too slow, so the fast simulator
mirrors the same economics in floating point. Its agent is marginal. The
position never moves the price, and its fee share is the fraction of the active
liquidity it supplies. The simulator can run on the recorded events or on a
calibrated regime-switching jump process fitted to the training years, and it
executes a decision at the price observed before the interval advances, as a
live LP would.

Two numeric worlds are only useful if their disagreement is bounded. The parity
harness feeds both engines the same prices, positions and gas series, then
compares fees, impermanent loss and PnL under a stated tolerance. The
simulator's fee engine was made exact, so the remaining drift on real data is
tiny. Mean fee differences sit on the order of $10^{-7}$ USDC per step per
seed, and impermanent loss matches exactly. The harness first exercised only
the passive policy, which never rebalances. It now also runs a policy that
rebalances, which is the setting the fee and loss results depend on. A separate suite of
look-ahead probes attacks the composed system at the train/eval seam, where a
leak would be easiest to hide.

\subsection{The failures that shaped the design}

The first two training runs produced policies that did nothing useful. The
seeds collapsed to one deterministic action, some holding throughout and others
repeating a single rebalance, and the measured advantage barely separated the
actions. The collapse was not a tuning problem. Table~\ref{tab:repairs} lists
the interface defects behind it and the repair for each. Individually the
repairs are small. Together they were the difference between a policy that can
learn and one that cannot, which is why the failure is treated as a result
rather than an implementation note.
