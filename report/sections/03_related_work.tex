\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

The study sits between two literatures. The first prices what an automated
market maker can earn and lose; it shows that adverse selection is a
structural cost, not an accident of who trades. The second supplies the
decision machinery: reinforcement learning for market making and trading, and
the risk-sensitive extensions that judge a policy on its tail. This section
reviews both, states what each establishes, and locates the gap the study
fills.

\subsection{Market making and optimal execution}

Classical market making quotes prices around a reference value while managing
inventory risk. Avellaneda and Stoikov model a dealer who posts bid and ask
quotes against a limit order book, derives reservation prices from inventory
and risk aversion, and shows how spreads widen as inventory grows and as the
horizon shortens\cite{avellaneda2008high}. The same tension, between capturing
the spread and carrying unwanted inventory, governs a much older problem:
Almgren and Chriss choose a liquidation schedule that trades price impact
against timing risk, and the optimal schedule is the ancestor of the
cost-aware rebalancing rule this study uses as a benchmark\cite{almgren2001optimal}.
Neither model is an AMM. A limit order book dealer chooses quote prices and
sizes, while a liquidity provider chooses a price range and a liquidity share;
the LP's inventory is a deterministic function of the range, and its loss
mechanism is the concavity of that function rather than an adverse selection
spread.

The reinforcement learning line replaces closed-form quotes with learned
policies. Gu\'eant and Manziuk train a policy that quotes across hundreds of
corporate bonds at once, sidestepping the curse of dimensionality that makes a
per-instrument model impractical\cite{gueant2019deep}. Ganesh et al.\ build a
multi-agent dealer market and show that a learner discovers competitor pricing
and learns to shade quotes and manage inventory, with the reward
specification deciding much of the behaviour\cite{ganesh2019reinforcement}.
Zhang et al.\ study continuous futures with volatility-scaled positions and
report gains against time-series momentum after transaction
costs\cite{zhang2019deep}. These papers demonstrate that reinforcement
learning works in trading settings with realistic frictions, and they supply
the reward design lessons, but their state is an order book and their action
is a quote, so they do not speak to range geometry or to the losses that make
liquidity provision hard.

\subsection{Automated market maker design and LP economics}

The mechanics of constant-function market makers are well mapped. Angeris et
al.\ prove that a constant-product market tracks the reference price under mild
conditions and characterise the deviation when the reference moves away, with
agent-based simulations supporting the stability argument\cite{angeris2019analysis}.
The same authors give conditions under which a constant-function market works
as a price oracle\cite{angeris2020improved}, and later work axiomatises the
family and shows that every constant-function market admits a unique
well-behaved trading function in a general geometric
framework\cite{schlegel2022axioms,angeris2023geometry}. Xu et al.\ systematise
the design space and its trade-offs\cite{xu2021sok}. This literature explains
what a pool does to prices; it treats the liquidity provider's return as a
residual of the mechanism.

That residual is now measured. Capponi and Jia show that automated execution
transfers value from liquidity providers to arbitrageurs when exchange rates
move, that adoption depends on the pair's usefulness and on the correlation of
its legs, and that a more curved pricing curve blunts arbitrage at the cost of
trader surplus\cite{capponi2021adoption}. Heimbach et al.\ model the choices a
Uniswap~v3 provider faces and argue that the design demands far more decisions
than earlier automated markets, with fee income and loss exposure moving
together\cite{heimbach2022risks}. Loesch et al.\ measure impermanent loss on
live v3 positions and show that concentration magnifies it when the price
leaves the band\cite{loesch2021impermanent}. Fritsch and Canidio compare the
two sides on real pools and find arbitrage losses typically exceeding the fees
earned, with passive full-range positions often outperforming concentrated
ones\cite{fritsch2024measuring}.

The theory of that loss is the anchor of this report. Milionis et al.\ define
loss-versus-rebalancing as the gap between the pool and a continuously
rebalanced portfolio, derive the rate $\frac{1}{8}\sigma^2V$ for a full-range
constant-product position, and show that it accrues on any price path, whether
or not any trader is informed\cite{milionis2022lvr}. Fees change the
threshold rather than the mechanism: with a proportional fee, arbitrage only
pays once the pool price deviates by more than the fee, and the expected
arbitrage profit scales down with the fraction of blocks that present a
profitable opportunity\cite{milionis2023fees}. Competition among permissionless
providers then pushes liquidity past the social optimum, because each provider
ignores the adverse-selection cost its entry imposes on the
others\cite{ma2024cost}. Campbell et al.\ endogenise the fee itself and show
that the profit-maximising fee depends on volatility and volume, so the fee
that clears an LP's costs is a function of the market state rather than a
protocol constant\cite{campbell2025optimal}. The empirical and the theoretical
strands agree on the shape of the problem: gross fee yield is not an LP's
return, and the gap between them is adverse selection.

What this literature does not supply is a decision rule. Its objects are
mechanisms, fee schedules and passive positions; the provider is a price taker
of the mechanism, not an agent who chooses when to act, how wide to sit and
how much of the residual risk to hedge.

\subsection{Learning for liquidity provision}

A smaller literature learns the provider's decisions. Zhang et al.\ train a
hedged double deep Q-network on a v3 environment and report large gains over
baselines, but the hedge is assumed frictionless and the policy sees no regime
features\cite{zhang2023adaptive}. ZeroSwap learns the spread of a market maker
so that quoted prices track an external reference, an orthogonal lever to
range placement\cite{nadkarni2023zeroswap}. Chionas et al.\ treat concentrated
liquidity as a risk-aversion problem in simulation and find that higher
volatility and gas widen optimal positions and reduce rebalancing, while a
risk penalty trims the adverse tail; their analysis uses no on-chain data and
no hedge\cite{chionas2026concentrated}. Xu and Brini train a PPO provider that
adjusts positions on rolling windows to balance fees against impermanent loss
under regime shifts\cite{xu2025improving}. RAmmStein poses regime-dependent
rebalancing thresholds as a problem of impulse control and reports that lazy
rebalancing when the regime warrants it improves net return with far fewer
rebalances, though its net metric excludes impermanent loss and the study
relies on off-chain data for one pair\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}.

These studies establish that a learned provider can beat a hand-written rule
inside their respective settings. Each leaves at least one of the study's
requirements untouched: none charges every friction on both the liquidity and
hedge legs, none evaluates on validated on-chain replay across several seeds
with paired intervals, and none places the hedge ratio in the same learned
action as the range decision.

\subsection{Risk-sensitive reinforcement learning}

Judging a policy on its tail is a distinct problem from maximising its mean.
Mihatsch and Neuneier replace the expectation in the Bellman recursion with an
exponential utility and show that a single parameter interpolates between
risk-seeking and risk-averse behaviour\cite{mihatsch2002risk}. Tamar et al.\
extend policy gradients to coherent risk measures generally, using a
convex-programming step over the risk envelope\cite{tamar2015policy}, and Chow
and Ghavamzadeh derive policy-gradient and actor-critic algorithms for
mean-CVaR Markov decision processes, which makes the tail a differentiable
objective rather than a post-hoc measurement\cite{chow2014algorithms}. B\"uhler
et al.\ bring the same machinery to hedging under frictions and risk limits,
and show that a rich enough set of learned strategies approximates the optimal
solution\cite{buehler2018deep}. The measures themselves are fixed by
Artzner et al.'s coherence axioms\cite{artzner1999coherent} and by the convex
representation of Rockafellar and Uryasev\cite{rockafellar2000cvar}.

This study uses those results in two places. The reward subtracts a multiple
of a trailing empirical expected shortfall, so the policy's gradient sees the
tail it will be judged on, and the checkpoint selection ranks candidates by a
validation Sharpe computed on a held-out slice of the training lane. The
second choice is a direct response to the failure documented in the results:
selection on training return chose an over-hedged policy that lost out of
sample.

\subsection{Learning methods and tooling}

The optimization machinery is standard and deliberately so.
Reinforcement learning solves sequential decisions from interaction data
without a model\cite{sutton2018reinforcement}; proximal policy optimization
supplies a clipped surrogate objective that is stable across
updates\cite{schulman2017ppo}; generalized advantage estimation controls the
variance of its advantage estimates\cite{schulman2015gae}. The study uses the
reference implementations of both through Stable-Baselines3 on the Gymnasium
interface\cite{raffin2021stablebaselines3,towers2024gymnasium}, which keeps
the experiments reproducible without making the algorithms part of the
contribution. The one interface that is not standard is the action space: the
policy emits a factored action whose first component is a range choice and
whose second is a hedge ratio, a discrete multi-head space rather than a
single categorical output.

\subsection{The gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} places the closest studies beside this one. Two patterns
are visible. The economic literature prices the LP's losses but leaves the
decisions open; the learning literature makes the decisions but evaluates them
in simulation, on one leg, or without the frictions that decide the sign of
the result. This study trains a single policy over width and hedge on recorded
on-chain events, charges gas, slippage, fees and funding on both legs, and
reports the pre-registered risk comparison across five seeds with paired
intervals.
