\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{The economics of passive liquidity}

The modern account of AMM LP returns begins with adverse selection. Milionis et
al.\ characterise the loss a constant-product LP suffers relative to a
continuously rebalanced portfolio, name it loss-versus-rebalancing, and derive
the local rate $\frac{1}{8}\sigma^2 V$ for a full-range position; the loss does
not require informed traders to be modeled explicitly, since any price path
generates it, and protocol fees and the choice of curve shift the
balance~\cite{milionis2022lvr}. Their framework implies a viability condition
that we test against recorded pool histories in C1: fee income must exceed the
LVR rate.

Whether that condition holds in practice has been tested on-chain. Fritsch and
Canidio measure realized arbitrage losses against fee income across many large
AMM pools and find that the losses typically dominate; in their sample a
passive full-range position would have fared better than concentrated
alternatives, and even a v2 deposit would have outperformed v3 in several
pools~\cite{fritsch2024measuring}. Loesch et al.\ study v3 positions directly
and show that concentrating liquidity magnifies impermanent loss when the price
leaves the band: a narrow position can lose more than a v2 deposit over the
same path~\cite{loesch2021impermanent}. Undertow replays each pool's own
events, so the fee and impermanent loss terms in C1 come from the accumulators
the protocol wrote.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for liquidity provision}

Several recent papers apply RL to the LP problem, each isolating a different
lever. Zhang et al.\ train a hedged double deep Q-network agent and report
improvements of $9$--$69\,\%$ over baselines, but their hedge is assumed
frictionless (their Assumptions~2--3), the policy receives no regime features,
and the evaluation spans four test periods; they also document that the
\emph{unhedged} agent's PnL changes sign with the market trend, which is
precisely the directional risk a hedge is meant to
remove~\cite{zhang2023adaptive}. ZeroSwap takes an orthogonal route: instead of
choosing where to place liquidity, it learns the spread of a market maker so
that quoted prices track an external reference. In their competitive regime
with zero profit the learned spread is the quantity that matters, and a
static constant-product curve converges to the reference considerably more
slowly~\cite{nadkarni2023zeroswap}. RAmmStein derives rebalancing thresholds
that depend on the regime as a problem of impulse control: greedy rebalancing
loses $8.4\,\%$ in their setting, while a policy that rebalances lazily when the
regime warrants it gains $1.6\,\%$ net ROI with roughly $85\,\%$ fewer
rebalances. Their net metric excludes impermanent loss, the study covers a
single pair, and it relies on off-chain market
data~\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}. Chionas et al.\ study concentrated liquidity
as a problem of risk aversion in simulation: higher volatility and higher gas
push optimal positions wider and reduce rebalancing, and an objective that
penalizes risk trims the adverse tail of the return distribution. Their
analysis is simulation only, uses no on-chain data, and does not consider a
hedge leg~\cite{chionas2026concentrated}.

A separate line of work learns market making quotes in the classical limit
order book, where the spread and inventory are the control variables and the
objective is typically a trade-off between mean and variance over inventory
risk~\cite{avellaneda2008high}. That formulation supplies the risk-sensitive
vocabulary we use, but its state is a lit order book and its actions are
quotes, not range geometry.

\subsection{Tools and evaluation}

We use Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with clipping as the learning
algorithm~\cite{schulman2017ppo}, implemented through Stable-Baselines3 on
Gymnasium environments~\cite{raffin2021stablebaselines3,towers2024gymnasium}.
For evaluation under risk we use CVaR at the $5\,\%$ level, a coherent risk
measure optimizable through its Rockafellar--Uryasev convex
representation~\cite{rockafellar2000cvar}, alongside the annualized Sharpe
ratio.

\subsection{Positioning}

Across this literature the pieces exist separately: the economics of LVR, the
idea of learning a rebalance policy, the idea of a hedge, and the idea of risk
aversion. A single study that trains over the \emph{joint} action of rebalance
timing, band width and hedge ratio, charges every friction on both legs, and
evaluates on \emph{validated} on-chain replay under an explicitly
risk-sensitive metric has not been established. Undertow is built to close that
gap. Its exact backtester makes the fee and LVR accounting a measured quantity
rather than a modeling assumption, its float64 simulator makes the joint
action space trainable, and the parity harness between them bounds the gap from
simulation to reality that earlier studies which only simulate leave
open~\cite{chionas2026concentrated}. The rest of this paper formalizes the MDP
(Section~\ref{sec:formulation}), describes the testbed
(Section~\ref{sec:system}), and reports the four claims of
Section~\ref{sec:intro} in order (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
