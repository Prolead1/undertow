\section{Background: Mechanical and Economic Setting}
\label{sec:background}

The pair is \textsf{USDC/WETH}: token0 is USDC and token1 is WETH, as in the
on-chain pools we use. Protocol quantities are raw Uniswap quantities: $s$
denotes the raw square root of the pool price ($\sqrt{P}$, increasing in tick)
and $L$ the
raw liquidity. The ETH price in USD is
\begin{equation}
q(s) \;=\; \frac{10^{\,d_1-d_0}}{s^2},
\label{eq:humanprice}
\end{equation}
roughly \SI{3000}{USDC/WETH} at $s \approx 1.8\times 10^{4}$, with token
decimals $d_0 = 6$ and $d_1 = 18$; because $q$ decreases in $s$, a lower tick
means a higher ETH price. Monetary quantities are always converted to USDC, and
the decimal factors cancel from every ratio we report.

\subsection{Concentrated liquidity in Uniswap v3}

Uniswap~v3 replaces a single constant-product curve with a family of curves
indexed by integer \emph{ticks}~\cite{adams2021uniswap}. An LP selects a tick
interval $[t_\ell, t_u]$, equivalently a raw band $[s_a, s_b]$ with
$s_a < s_b$, and deposits liquidity $L$ that backs trades only while the pool
price stays inside the band. For $s \in [s_a, s_b]$, the position holds
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
x(s) &= \frac{L}{10^{d_0}}\!\left(\frac{1}{s}-\frac{1}{s_b}\right), \\
y(s) &= \frac{L}{10^{d_1}}\!\left(s-s_a\right),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:amounts}
\end{equation}
in human token units, so its USDC value is
\begin{equation}
V(s) \;=\; x(s) + y(s)\,q(s)
\;=\; \frac{L}{10^{d_0}}\!\left(\frac{2}{s}-\frac{1}{s_b}
-\frac{s_a}{s^2}\right).
\label{eq:value}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:value} makes the payoff structure visible. As the ETH price
falls ($s$ rises) toward the band, the position rotates into WETH; as the price
rises ($s$ falls), it rotates into USDC. At $s = s_b$ it holds only WETH, and
above the band it is a pure WETH holding whose USDC value falls as the ETH
price falls; symmetrically, at $s = s_a$ it holds only USDC. A v3 position
therefore has a truncated, concave payoff in the price, in effect a short
straddle around the current tick: it sells the asset that is appreciating and
buys the one that is depreciating, and it stops doing so once the price leaves
the band.

Fee income accrues pro rata to the liquidity a position contributes at each
tick crossed. The pool records cumulative fee growth per unit of liquidity, so
a position that is in range over an interval earns $L \cdot \Delta\phi$ and a
position that is out of range earns nothing~\cite{adams2021uniswap}. A narrow
band raises the LP's share of active liquidity for a given deposit, which
multiplies fee density, but it shortens the expected time until the price exits
and hence increases the frequency of costly recentring. This tension between
fee density and band lifetime is the heart of the control problem.

\subsection{Impermanent loss and loss-versus-rebalancing}

A passive LP's return is not the pool's fee yield; it is fee income net of the
change in the position's value relative to simply holding the initial deposit.
For a full-range constant-product position initially split evenly in value, if
the ETH price ratio is $r = q_t / q_0$, the position is worth
$\rho(r) = 2\sqrt{r}/(1+r)$ relative to the initial basket, so the
\emph{impermanent loss} (IL) is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{IL}(r) \;=\; \frac{2\sqrt{r}}{1+r} - 1
\;=\; -\frac{1}{8}(r-1)^2 + O\!\left((r-1)^3\right),
\label{eq:il}
\end{equation}
which is second order in the price move; for example,
$\mathrm{IL}(1.2) = -0.41\,\%$. On v3 the same formula applies only inside the
band; outside it the position holds only one of the two assets and the
comparison depends on where the band was
placed~\cite{loesch2021impermanent}.

IL measures the loss relative to the LP's own initial mix, but there is a
sharper benchmark. Suppose a portfolio continuously rebalances to hold the same
instantaneous token0 exposure as the LP position. Expanding the full-range
value function $V(P) = 2\sqrt{kP}$ (in token1 units, with constant product
$k$) to second order shows that the LP underperforms that portfolio, which
replicates the LP's delta, at the rate
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{dLVR}_t \;=\; \frac{1}{8}\,\sigma^2\, V_t \,\mathrm{d}t ,
\label{eq:lvr}
\end{equation}
which is the \emph{loss-versus-rebalancing} of Milionis et
al.~\cite{milionis2022lvr}. Adverse selection therefore compounds with the
square of volatility and linearly with the value at risk, and fee income must
exceed it for passive provision to be viable. Concentrating liquidity raises
the fee density but not the fee rate, so it improves the LP's position only
when the extra fee share outweighs the extra exposure to arbitrage. The
empirical studies in Section~\ref{sec:related} find that, on many large pools,
it does not.

\subsection{The liquidity provider's control problem}

The quantities above describe a static position. In practice an LP manages a
sequence of decisions. Each recentring is an \emph{impulse}: it incurs a fixed
gas cost, a pool fee on the inventory imbalance that must be swapped to build
the new range, and slippage. The impulse resets the band's geometry, and with
it the fee density and the LVR exposure. The LP's choices are therefore (i)
\emph{when} to recenter, (ii) \emph{where} and \emph{how wide} to place the next
band, and (iii) \emph{how much market risk to carry}, either by holding the
endogenous direction of the position or by hedging it with a short perpetual
futures position whose notional tracks the LP's delta.

Two features make this a problem of impulse control rather than a static
optimization~\cite{anchuri2026rammstein}. First, the cost of acting is lumpy:
a rebalance is worth doing only when the expected improvement in fee income,
band lifetime or risk exceeds a threshold. Second, the state that determines
whether to act is only partially observed: the volatility regime is latent,
order flow is adversarial, and the LP sees history through delayed, aggregated
on-chain events. Risk preferences enter as much as expected return does. An LP
that maximizes expected fees will accept positions whose return distribution
has a heavy left tail; an LP averse to risk will trade expected return for a
compressed tail. We therefore evaluate policies by \emph{annualized Sharpe
ratio} and by \emph{conditional value at risk at the $5\,\%$ level} (CVaR), a
coherent risk measure expressible as a convex
optimization~\cite{rockafellar2000cvar}, and we report raw PnL and
excess-versus-HODL alongside them. Section~\ref{sec:formulation} turns this
description into a formal MDP.
